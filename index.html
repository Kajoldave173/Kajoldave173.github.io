PROJECT_01 FEATURED

LGD modeling & expected loss estimation

A two-stage loss-given-default model on Freddie Mac mortgage data that estimates lifetime expected loss inside a PD x LGD x EAD framework, deployed end to end with a model registry, CI/CD, and an explainable scoring API.

THE PROBLEM

Loss given default is one of the hardest quantities in credit risk to model honestly, because its distribution is bimodal. When a loan defaults, the lender usually recovers either almost everything or almost nothing, and the middle is thin. A single regression smooths that shape into a meaningless average, and it gets the tails wrong, which is precisely where capital reserves and loss provisions are decided. The goal was to respect that bimodality and produce a calibrated lifetime loss number the business could actually reserve against.

APPROACH

  • Built a two-stage model on more than 1 million Freddie Mac single-family loan records. Stage one is a logistic regime classifier that separates total-loss defaults from partial-recovery defaults. Stage two models severity within the partial-recovery regime.
  • Used Beta Regression for the severity stage, since loss severity lives on the zero-to-one interval and is naturally beta-distributed, and benchmarked it against an XGBoost severity model to test whether nonlinearity bought anything. The recovery-regime fit landed at an R squared of 0.25, consistent with what the published LGD literature reports for this kind of data.
  • Engineered the drivers that credit risk teams actually trust: DTI, original and current loan-to-value, and delinquency history, transformed through WoE binning with IV-based selection so the inputs stay interpretable and stable across origination cohorts.
  • Validated the way a model risk function would want it validated: decile-level calibration plots, Brier score decomposition into reliability and resolution, and vintage backtesting across the 2019, 2020, and 2021 cohorts.

WHAT IT FOUND

Across a portfolio carrying 246.82 billion dollars of unpaid principal balance, the model estimated 1.08 billion dollars of lifetime expected loss. More useful than the headline number was the concentration: the highest-risk decile carried 39 percent of expected loss while representing only 14 percent of loans. That is the kind of result that maps straight onto a watch list and a capital allocation decision, not just a model report.

DEPLOYMENT

I containerized both stages with Docker and deployed to AWS EC2. MLflow tracks runs and the Model Registry handles stage-gated promotion, so a model only advances if it clears the calibration thresholds. GitHub Actions runs the CI/CD pipeline, and a FastAPI endpoint serves scores with SHAP attributions attached, so a downstream user sees why a loan landed where it did, not just the figure.

PROJECT_02 FEATURED

Credit card fraud detection system

An end-to-end fraud detection pipeline on 590K transactions with severe class imbalance, framed not as a single accuracy number but as a tunable business decision between catching fraud and burning investigator time.

THE PROBLEM

Fraud detection is a class-imbalance problem first and a modeling problem second. With a 3.4 percent fraud rate, roughly 28 legitimate transactions for every fraudulent one, accuracy is a useless metric: a model that flags nothing scores 96 percent. The real question is how much fraud you can catch before the false positives flood the investigation queue, so the entire project was built around precision-recall, not accuracy, and around making the tradeoff a dial that fraud operations can turn.

APPROACH

  • Benchmarked four models, Logistic Regression, Random Forest, XGBoost, and LightGBM, under a time-based train and test split so the evaluation never leaks future information into the past, which is the most common way fraud models look great in testing and fail in production.
  • LightGBM won, beating XGBoost by 0.005 PR-AUC while training roughly four times faster, 105 seconds against 455, and with a smaller overfitting gap. Final model reached 0.577 PR-AUC and 0.92 ROC-AUC, a 16.9 times lift over the no-skill baseline.
  • Engineered 504 features from raw transaction and identity data, including smoothed target encoding for high-cardinality fields like email domains and addresses, parsing of device strings into 8 structured features, 156 paired missingness flags, and correlation-based pruning of 87 of 339 anonymized V-features at a 0.95 threshold.
  • Resolved a validation divergence under naive class weighting by sweeping LightGBM's scale_pos_weight from 1 to 10; the final model converged at 1,582 trees with a controlled 0.335 train-test PR-AUC gap.

FRAMING IT AS A DECISION

Rather than picking a threshold and hiding it, I produced a precision-recall threshold table so the cutoff is a business choice. At a 0.5 threshold the model catches 1,644 of 3,611 fraud cases, 45.5 percent recall, at 72.8 percent precision. Fraud operations can slide that dial against the real cost tradeoff between chargebacks and investigation hours. On top of that, SHAP global and local explanations surface the top fraud drivers and produce per-transaction reason codes for analyst review and regulatory explainability.

DEPLOYMENT

Tracked more than 20 experiments in MLflow on DagsHub with a model registry and a 0.005 PR-AUC promotion gate, so retraining cannot ship a model that only looks better because of noise. Deployed a Streamlit inference app on HuggingFace Spaces with a public demo, loading 10 fitted transformation artifacts so a single transaction is scored with exactly the same preprocessing as training. Containerized the service with Docker and authored Airflow DAGs for daily retraining and batch scoring.
